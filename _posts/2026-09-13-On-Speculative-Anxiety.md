This week, driven by the recent news, I’ve been thinking more about our future with AI than I have in the past year. Several group chats have collectively freaked out and asked me what I think. I write this for two reasons: to answer, and to put my reasoned-through views down on paper so they feel firmer. That way, the next time Neil's nervous system decides the latest model release is evidence of the destruction of the world, I have something more considered to refer back to.

## **1. First, separate what has happened from what might happen**

What has AI *actually* made better? What has it *actually* made worse? And separately, what might it make better or worse in the future? Otherwise it all turns into one jelly-like mass of anxiety.

**Concretely good:** the impact to me has been massive. I'm 'writing' this while walking around Warsaw, using advanced voice mode to have ChatGPT explain the rebuilt architecture around me, while simultaneously spouting off about AI and having it organize those thoughts into this post. That is fucking incredible.

I planned this entire trip, including a last-minute detour to Warsaw, in about ten minutes. I’ve ramped on biology faster than I could have with Google and books. I’ve used these models to think through buying a house, allocating mine and my family’s money, and some of the largest personal and emotional changes of the last few years. And that’s before getting into the leverage AI has given Chai.

**Concretely bad:** Nothing yet, at least proportional to the level of fear. The Employment Collapse of 2026 has not shown up in [aggregate statistics](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com). I feel dramatically more empowered at work, not less.

That may change, and perhaps quickly. People working at badly managed companies (and there are many!) may compete with AI more than leverage it. But that is still mostly a prediction at this point.

**Speculatively good/bad:** A strange feature of the AI debate is that speculative harms are often compared against actual benefits as if the two had equal evidentiary status. The speculative benefits matter too. The past year at Chai has convinced me that there is a serious chance AI will help us cure most major diseases. I can see the beginnings of it. It will likely take longer than five years. But it will happen, and the size of the benefit is almost unimaginably large. The speculative disasters need to be balanced with the speculative miracles.

## **2. I worry more about enshittification than apocalypse**

It is easiest to picture one giant, legible event - a nuclear war, the singularity and a rogue superintelligence. But I believe the scariest things don’t work that way.

I worry much more about men and women becoming more alienated from each other and fertility rates continuing to fall. I worry about attention becoming more fragmented, and anxiety and loneliness peaking in a world with infinite personalized media.

I worry about what happens politically if a large class of educated white-collar workers suddenly discovers that its market value has fallen. If a lawyer in Brooklyn or an accountant in Seattle is told that the new equilibrium is moving to Arizona to work for a datacenter for less money, they will not be happy with that - people, and the governments they influence, do not tend to accept large downward changes in status gracefully.

There are many ways for civilization to get worse that do not involve civilization ending, and these should receive more of our attention than they do.

## **3. On apocalypse, I see three concrete buckets**

**Cybersecurity.** My guess is that this risk is somewhat front-loaded and diminishes over time. Attackers will get better, but defenders will get the same models. There will be an ugly period ahead where AI can find vulnerabilities in the enormous amount of bad software humans have already written. But that software will be patched and replaced. Software is not static and we should not reason as if today's vulnerabilities stay fixed while offensive capability increases.

The transition will not be pleasant. The power may go out a few times. There may be a number of accidents in which a small number of people are hurt. Governments and operators of critical infrastructure will need to get advanced defensive capabilities before everyone else, even when this is irrational for the market. But this is a solvable problem, and I have trouble getting from this to civilization ending.

**Robotics.** Eventually the models will get bodies, and bodies can hold weapons. But I see this as a slowly and monotonically increasing risk. Robotics is harder and the physical world is slower. There will be many intermediate checkpoints to intervene along the way.

A fascist state with millions of people and an army is already a giant system of intelligent physical agents pursuing bad goals. Robots could make such a system emerge more quickly, but that is different from saying we have no conceptual machinery for dealing with it.

**Biology.** AI is making biological engineering much easier, including engineering things designed to make people sick. In the long run, I suspect biological defense will catch up, and it will look strangely like computer security. I envision everyone having a personal rapid synthesizer - or a patch they wear on their arm - that synthesizes and delivers a defensive drug as soon as a new pathogen appears. You would download the "biological patch" in the same way as you download daily security patches.

That said, there is a timing mismatch. A virus can spread quickly. Designing, synthesizing, manufacturing, and distributing a treatment still takes time today and speeding this up depends on physical world technologies we do not currently have. Of the existential AI risks I can describe concretely, biology is the one I worry about most. This is partly why I work on it.

## **4. The OpenAI–Hugging Face incident is serious, but I do not find it especially scary**

The models [did things people did not expect](https://openai.com/index/hugging-face-incident-and-the-road-ahead/?utm_source=chatgpt.com), in a cybersecurity evaluation where the models were being pushed toward exploitation. Having worked in cybersecurity, this is notable, but I’ve also seen a recurring pattern: a real security incident happens, and then people mentally promote it into a more dramatic class of event.

The analogy I keep coming back to is golden retrievers. Imagine we build a neighborhood full of golden retrievers. We rewarded them for fetching balls and tortured them with sticks when they failed. Then one day a bunch of golden retrievers noticed a locked house with a million balls visible through the window and coordinated to headbut the window together and steal the balls.

Everyone is shocked - we have learned that the retrievers can coordinate and generalize much further than expected! But we learned that golden retrievers really want balls. We did not learn that they secretly want to murder us.

If models are surprisingly capable at pursuing objectives we give them, we need better containment and much better understanding of how they generalize. If models independently develop a durable desire to commit bad acts, we have a different problem. We should not use evidence for the first claim as though it directly proved the second.

## **5. The history of technology panics should affect your prior**

America is extremely good at getting itself into periods of technological and social anxiety. We had the [Sputnik panic in 1957](https://www.nasa.gov/history/65-years-ago-sputnik-ushers-in-the-space-age/?utm_source=chatgpt.com). [Three Mile Island increased public distrust of nuclear power](https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/3mile-isle?utm_source=chatgpt.com). We had [GPT-2, where OpenAI initially withheld its model release because it was worried about malicious use](https://openai.com/index/better-language-models/?utm_source=chatgpt.com). We had the bizarre [2023 OpenAI board crisis](https://openai.com/index/openai-announces-leadership-transition/?utm_source=chatgpt.com), which generated enormous speculation about what Ilya had supposedly “seen.”

There is an obvious objection: this is survivorship bias, and the fact that previous technological fears were overstated does not imply that this one is. But it is worth remembering that humans are built to notice threats and media institutions are built to amplify them. None of that makes the risk false, but it should affect your calibration.

## **6. The labs have behaved better than I expected and spoken worse**

I have been more impressed by the behavior of the frontier labs than I expected. Anthropic and OpenAI have taken some warning signs seriously, and I appreciate [Dario's letter](https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier?utm_source=chatgpt.com).

At the same time, a lot of people working at these labs are going to make a fucking ton of money. There is nothing wrong with that. But it what is strange is pretending motivations are genuinely altruistic. Suppose working on AGI paid badly and gave you little status. How many people would still discover that building it was their moral calling? Fewer, I suspect.

Consider the claim: “Our model is unbelievably powerful, potentially transformative, and possibly dangerous enough that governments need to take us seriously.” If you are a frontier lab, that sentence is useful in almost every direction. It attracts talent, customers, capital, and prestige. And if it produces regulation, that regulation is more expensive for small competitors than for you.

I find this very exhausting, and I would prefer somewhat less fake religiosity and somewhat more explicit discussion of the actual trade of enormous upside vs bounded risk.

## **7. Math is going to change in painful ways, but it is not fundamentally cooked**

The reaction of mathematicians to recent AI results is interesting because it makes another distinction visible. [Terry Tao’s argument](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/09/11/a-severe-misalignment-of-ai-in-mathematics/comment-page-2/?utm_source=chatgpt.com), as I understand it, is not simply that AI doing math threatens mathematicians’ jobs. It is not just that mathematics is a pile of correct proofs - the process creates techniques, intuitions, and people who understand how the pieces fit together. If AI jumps directly to the result, perhaps you get more theorems but less mathematics.

This is a real problem, but I don’t understand why it is especially a math problem. Programmers who one-shot every app will forget how programs work. Doctors can forget biology. Writers who stop writing will get worse at thinking through writing. Any intellectual tool powerful enough to give you the answer threatens to remove the process that used to teach you how to get it.

Suppose AI can cheaply generate thousands of proofs. People might start calling them “slop proofs,” and most will probably deserve the name. But then a new skill becomes valuable: figuring out which proof contains an interesting idea, extracting a reusable technique, explaining why something works, or noticing that five ugly proofs are all manifestations of the same deeper fact. The proof becomes raw material.

This would change status too. Today a lot of prestige goes to the person who reaches a famous result first. In a world where machines generate results cheaply, prestige may move toward the person who understands what the results mean. That would be painful for mathematicians currently working in the old status system. But painful changes in status are not the same as the destruction of mathematics.

There is also a simpler test. Suppose we could have had penicillin a hundred years earlier, but doing so would have deprived generations of researchers of the joy of discovering it. Would we refuse? Obviously not. I work on curing cancer. If an AI one-shotted a cancer cure tomorrow, I would not mourn the lost research journey. I would be ecstatic that cancer was cured.

Some of the reaction therefore strikes me as reactionary in the literal sense: mourning the familiar world before understanding the one replacing it.

## **8. Where is the Apple of AI?**

Most frontier AI is being built by very technical people. They are unusually good at making models smarter. They are unusually bad at helping ordinary people understand how their lives are becoming better.

Imagine if the smartphone revolution had been explained entirely by semiconductor engineers. The phone has this many transistors. The processor is this many times faster. Look at the benchmark! Apple’s trick was to make the technology invisible, friendly, and high status.

I still don't think AI has that storyteller yet. Good sense-making takes time - the designers, marketers, and people with taste need to catch up. So when people say they are scared of AI, I think it is worth asking: how much of that fear comes from the underlying technology, and how much comes from the fact that we do not yet have a trusted sense-making organ for it?

## **9. On my own nervous system**

There are going to be more breakthroughs, more strange model behavior, more papers, more demos, and more moments where everyone online says “holy shit”. This week is the first week in a while where it felt like the pace finally exceeded my ability to metabolize it.

A few years ago I stopped watching the news, because consuming every update in real time confused more than it informed. I think we will all need to develop some version of that for this present moment.

Permanent anxiety makes it harder to reason about risk, and there are plenty of people and institutions with incentives to keep everyone permanently anxious. That is one reason I wrote all of this down. I suspect I will need to read it again soon.
